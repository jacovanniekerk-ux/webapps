Statement 1True or False?

"AI tools like ChatGPT work the same way as Google — you search for something and it gives you links to pages that already exist on the internet."

FalseAI generates, it doesn't retrieve.

Google is like a library — you search a catalogue and find something that already exists. AI is more like a conversation with a librarian: you describe what you need, and it generates a brand new response in real time, built word by word based on patterns it has learned, rather than pulling an existing page or link.

Because AI is generating text rather than fetching it, it can't point you to a published source the way a search engine can.
Statement 2True or False?

"Because AI can hallucinate — stating incorrect information confidently as fact — teachers should always double-check anything AI-generated before using it in a lesson."

TrueHuman judgement stays in the loop.

AI doesn't "know" facts the way a person does — it generates responses based on patterns in its training data, and it can misunderstand an unclear question or simply make things up. This is why the teacher's professional judgement always needs to stay in control: AI can support your work, but it should never replace your own verification.

Treat AI output the way you'd treat a bright but overconfident colleague's first draft — useful, but worth checking.
Statement 3True or False?

"The wording of your prompt makes very little difference to the quality of the AI's response — the output is mostly a matter of luck."

FalsePrompting is king.

The clearer and more specific your instructions, the better the response you'll get. Asking AI to "explain osmosis" gives a generic answer, but adding context — the audience, the tone, the persona, the purpose — reshapes the output dramatically every time, even though the underlying topic stays the same.

Give AI context the way you'd brief a substitute teacher: who the audience is, what tone you want, and what the end goal looks like.
Statement 4True or False?

"AI has equally rich and accurate information about every text, so a prompt about a well-known international play and a prompt about a local South African setwork are just as likely to be reliable."

FalseData coverage isn't equal.

AI is trained on huge amounts of internet data, so it tends to have far more information on widely studied international works — like a Shakespeare play — than on lesser-documented local South African setworks. For that content, outputs are more likely to be thin or inaccurate, so it's especially important to check them against your own knowledge of the text and your learners' context.

The less "internet presence" a topic has, the more carefully you need to fact-check what AI gives you.
