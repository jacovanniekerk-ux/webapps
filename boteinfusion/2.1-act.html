Statement 1 True or False?

"The slides in your presentation are the main focal point of the lesson — they should contain as much information as possible so learners can follow along."

False The teacher is the focal point — not the screen.

As Jakob explained in the video, the teacher is always the focal point of any lesson. Everything on the screen is meant to support and supplement the teacher — not replace them. Overloading slides with text creates a problem: learners start reading the screen instead of listening to you. A presentation is not the same as learner notes.

Keep slides lean. If your slide makes complete sense without you speaking, it may be doing too much of your job.
Statement 2 True or False?

"Revealing slide content piece by piece — rather than all at once — helps reduce cognitive overload by keeping learner attention where you need it."

True This is the principle of triggering.

This is exactly what Jakob called triggering information. When an entire screen appears at once, learners' eyes wander to content you haven't spoken about yet — creating a split in attention. By revealing content progressively, you direct exactly where learners look and align it with what you are saying at that moment.

Use animations like "Appear on click" to build your slide content gradually during your explanation.
Statement 3 True or False?

"Signaling only refers to using digital annotation tools like arrows on screen — traditional pointers and laser clickers are no longer considered effective."

False Signaling includes both digital and traditional techniques.

Jakob made it clear that signaling includes many approaches — bold text, colour changes, annotation tools, drawn arrows, and even traditional physical tools like a long ruler or a laser-pointer clicker. These all serve the same purpose: guiding learner attention to the most important element on screen at a given moment. No method is outdated if it works.

Combine methods — a bold keyword on your slide plus a drawn circle using an annotation tool is more powerful than either alone.
Statement 4 True or False?

"Using a consistent template throughout your presentation is mainly a matter of personal taste and has no real effect on how learners process the content."

False Consistency reduces cognitive effort — it's not just aesthetic.

The video highlighted consistency as one of the three core aesthetic principles, alongside legibility and imagery. When fonts, colours, and graphics are consistent, learners don't waste mental effort adjusting to new visual styles — they can focus on the content. Pre-designed templates are specifically recommended because they enforce this consistency automatically.

Pick one template and one icon pack and stick to them across your entire lesson. Visual predictability actually frees up cognitive space for learning.
Statement 5 True or False?

"A minimum recommended font size for on-screen presentation text is 20pt, because anything smaller makes it difficult for learners to read from a distance."

True 20pt is the minimum — 28pt is even better.

Jakob specifically mentioned 20pt as the minimum recommended font size, with 28pt being even more effective for readability. Legibility was described as the starting point of everything — if learners cannot read the screen, nothing else matters. This doesn't mean you can't go smaller when you have a lot of content, but you should always aim as large as possible.

Test your slides by standing at the back of your classroom. If you struggle to read them, your learners in the back row definitely will too.
Statement 6 True or False?

"Using images and icons in your presentation is a nice-to-have but doesn't significantly affect how well learners remember the content."

False Research shows visuals significantly improve recall.

Jakob cited research showing that people are becoming increasingly visual learners, and that images create powerful memory anchors. When a learner connects an image to a concept during a lesson, they are more likely to recall that concept in an assessment — the image acts as a retrieval cue. This is particularly relevant given the visual-rich environment learners are exposed to (TikTok, Instagram, etc.).

Use consistent icons to represent recurring concepts — a familiar icon can become a mental shortcut that helps learners recall information during assessments.
